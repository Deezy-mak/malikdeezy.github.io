Forex signals, automated trading bots, crypto "opportunities," and social-media financial freedom pitches all share one trait: they ask you to hand over money based on trust and promised returns. Before you do, it pays to check who is actually behind the offer and whether they're allowed to operate.

## A documented example: Silver Star FX / Silver Star Live

In November 2019, the U.S. Commodity Futures Trading Commission (CFTC) issued an order settling charges against Silver Star FX, LLC (doing business as Silver Star Live), Silver Star Live Software LLC, and two of their officers, Candace Ross-Mahmoud and Hassan Mahmoud.

According to the CFTC, the two companies acted as unregistered commodity trading advisors, and the two individuals acted as unregistered associated persons. Between at least July 2018 and March 2019, the order found, they solicited customers to open discretionary trading accounts and offered to trade customers' retail forex accounts using an algorithm-driven "forex autotrader," which was marketed as "95% hands-off" after setup. The matter was resolved by settlement, with a $75,000 civil penalty and a requirement to cease and desist from future violations of the Commodity Exchange Act.

To be clear, this is a record of a regulator's findings in one settled case. It is not a claim that any other business or service connected to these names is fraudulent. The official release is here: [CFTC Release 8071-19](https://www.cftc.gov/PressRoom/PressReleases/8071-19).

## Red flags in forex, crypto, and trading-bot offers

- Promises of high or "guaranteed" returns with little or no risk
- "Hands-off" or "fully automated" profits that need no knowledge on your part
- Pressure to deposit quickly or recruit others
- Trading performance claims with no independently audited track record
- Vague or hard-to-meet withdrawal conditions
- No clear, checkable registration with a financial regulator

## How to verify before you send money

1. Search the company and the individuals in your financial regulator's registration database. In the U.S., use the National Futures Association's BASIC database. Elsewhere, use your national regulator's licensed-firm list.
2. Read the regulator's published warnings and enforcement actions for the names involved.
3. Ask where your funds are held and how withdrawals work, in writing.
4. Treat screenshots of profits as marketing, not proof.
5. Never invest money you can't afford to lose, and be especially cautious with leverage.

If you've lost money to a trading scheme, report it to your local financial regulator and, for U.S. matters, to the CFTC at cftc.gov.
